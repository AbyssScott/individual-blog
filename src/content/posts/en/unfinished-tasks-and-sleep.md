I came across an interesting article on the BBC today:

[*Your brain is exhausted from too many unfinished tasks. Five simple steps can help.*](https://www.bbc.co.uk/future/article/20261123-why-unfinished-tasks-exhaust-our-brains-and-how-to-let-them-go)

![BBC article: Your brain is exhausted from too many unfinished tasks](/individual-blog/images/unfinished-tasks-and-sleep-01.png)

<small>Source: BBC (cover photo: Getty Images).</small>

The article discusses a psychological phenomenon known as the **Zeigarnik effect**. In 1927, psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik published a paper suggesting that people tend to remember interrupted tasks better than completed ones. Although subsequent studies have not always replicated this memory effect, a number of studies suggest that unfinished tasks can continue to occupy our minds.

![BBC article excerpt on the Zeigarnik effect](/individual-blog/images/unfinished-tasks-and-sleep-02.png)

<small>Source: BBC.</small>

I can certainly relate to this.

I often get anxious when evening comes around and I still haven't finished what I'd planned to do that day (writing a paragraph of an essay, finishing a problem set, or getting through the material I meant to review). Even when I'm exhausted, I find it difficult to fall asleep. So I usually end up staying up until I get everything done. The downside, of course, is that if I don't have an early class the next morning, I have no idea how late I'll sleep; if I do, I'll just have to show up half asleep.

I know perfectly well that such an irregular sleep schedule is bad for my health. But that's exactly the problem: **I know I should go to bed earlier, yet I find it difficult to do so without feeling uneasy about the things I've left unfinished.**

So I wanted to see what the BBC article had to recommend. There are five suggestions:

1. **Set time-based goals**
2. **Switch off notifications**
3. **Where possible, avoid project overlap**
4. **Plan deadlines around holidays**
5. **'The doorway effect': Create transition rituals**

Well, at least in my experience, the first two suggestions aren't particularly easy to put into practice.

**On the first suggestion.** As I understand it, the idea is to decide how much time to spend working on something today rather than how much work I must get done.

But I think the problem here is that some tasks aren't so easily interrupted. Suppose I decide to spend three hours working on a paper every evening, from 8 to 11 p.m., and then go to bed. As luck would have it, **I spend the first two hours and forty minutes reading the literature without any clear idea of what my argument should be, only to come up with a promising one at 10:45 p.m.** But the argument isn't fully developed yet, and I might need another half hour to think through its logic and whether it's consistent with what I've written so far.

At this point, my plan to go to bed on time is probably out the window. On the one hand, I'm worried that after a night's sleep (or perhaps a dream), I'll have forgotten much of my train of thought and won't be able to pick it up again the next day. On the other hand, even if I jot down the argument and actually get into bed, I'll probably keep thinking about it anyway and end up unable to sleep. I've found myself in this situation many times.

**On the second suggestion.** Well, I never turn off my email notifications. Especially now that I'm in graduate school, I care a lot about seeing important emails promptly. I'm also well aware that, for the vast majority of emails, seeing them a few hours later or even the next day probably makes no difference. Yet somehow I just can't be comfortable with that.

I don't have much to say about the third and fourth suggestions, since juggling multiple projects and dealing with a bunch of deadlines over the holidays aren't problems I regularly encounter at the moment. So I'll skip those.

**The fifth suggestion** involves something called the *doorway effect*. The article suggests using transition rituals to mentally separate different tasks or to switch from work mode to rest mode.

This actually sounds somewhat useful. It strikes me as the psychological version of “go outside, clear your head, get a change of scenery, and stop sitting in your room all day.” Sometimes, when I get anxious about my work, I do take a walk around campus.

But I suspect that what I need isn't more advice on how to go to bed earlier. What I really need is to **learn to be genuinely okay with the fact that the day is over and some things are still unfinished.**
