While reading *The Economist*, I came across an article titled [*A new axis of malice threatens the global order*](https://www.economist.com/interactive/international/2026/11/19/a-new-axis-of-malice-threatens-the-global-order). Judging by the title, the article seems to suggest that China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea are forming some sort of evil and morally unjust alliance, one that all the righteous people of the world ought to unite against.

To be honest, the title annoyed me a little. After all, as a Chinese person, I can't help feeling somewhat offended when my country is described as part of an "axis of evil." It almost makes me feel as though I'm part of that evil myself. But I don't think either I or my country is evil.

Apart from being annoyed, though, I had another question: what exactly is so "new" about this axis? As far as I can remember, calling China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea an "axis of evil" is hardly anything new, even if I don't particularly like the label. For example, back in October 2023, US Senator Mitch McConnell had already publicly described these four countries as an "axis of evil" ([original CBS interview](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/mitch-mcconnell-senate-minority-leader-face-the-nation-transcript-10-22-2023/)). I find it hard to believe that the authors at *The Economist* were unaware of this.

Anyway, I still wanted to take a closer look at why *The Economist* considers these countries, including China, to be "evil" in the first place.

<figure>
  <img src="/individual-blog/images/evil-02-cooperation.png" alt="Figure 2: The Economist on the four countries' military and economic co-operation" />
  <figcaption>Figure 2. The Economist on military, technological, and economic cooperation among the four countries.</figcaption>
</figure>

Well... judging by this passage (Figure 2), *The Economist* seems to think that one important reason this axis is evil is that these countries have somehow dared to cooperate with one another, sharing military technology and expertise, deepening economic and industrial cooperation, and thereby changing the balance of military power in Europe, the Middle East, and Asia.

I'm not entirely convinced by this argument. For example, I imagine China and plenty of other countries would be delighted if the United States were willing to share its advanced military technology and expertise, such as advanced fighter jets, long-range missiles, and military chips. After all, these technologies look pretty cool, and they could certainly help strengthen a country's defense capabilities. Of course, the United States has no obligation to share them with China. But if it doesn't want to share them, and doesn't want other countries to do so either, I don't find it particularly surprising that China might look for other partners.

The same seems to apply to economic cooperation. Remember how the Trump administration handled tariffs on China in 2025? I wouldn't exactly call that an invitation to deepen economic cooperation with China.

So I suspect that closer military, technological, and economic cooperation among China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea may partly reflect Western restrictions on cooperation with these countries in certain areas. Of course, that's only one of many possible explanations. But if you restrict cooperation with these countries while also describing their cooperation with one another as some kind of evil activity, the message seems to be: you're not allowed to cooperate with us, and preferably you shouldn't cooperate with each other either. Perhaps you should all just disappear.

Uh... anyway, I think that's pretty dumb.

As for changing the balance of power... well, I suspect part of the argument may concern the regional balance between Israel and Iran. I don't particularly think Russia and China have done anything especially evil in this regard. Still, perhaps the authors are referring to military weapons, components, or technical support that these countries may have provided to Iran. I don't rule out that possibility.

But here's the problem. First, is Israel some poor, helpless child receiving no assistance from Western countries? Not as far as I know. The United States has long provided Israel with substantial military assistance, including advanced weapons and cooperation on missile defense.

I suppose someone might respond that Israel represents the forces of good, while Iran represents the forces of evil. Therefore, helping the good guys is good, and helping the bad guys is evil. Well, if that's the argument, I guess there's not much I can say in response.

Second, there's also Iraq. In 2003, Iraq was "liberated," and Saddam Hussein's regime was overthrown. One important consequence was that a regional power capable of counterbalancing Iran was severely weakened, allowing Iran's influence in the region to expand. This is hardly a novel observation in discussions of Middle Eastern geopolitics. If changing the regional balance of power is itself something to be condemned, then I'm not sure why the United States' actions back then apparently escape the same standard of judgment.

The article then turns to the global nuclear balance (Figure 3).

<figure>
  <img src="/individual-blog/images/evil-03-nuclear.png" alt="Figure 3: The Economist on the global nuclear balance" />
  <figcaption>Figure 3. The Economist on the global nuclear balance.</figcaption>
</figure>

This part also seems rather problematic. The article says that, according to Western officials, the Kremlin is helping China build *“a missile-warning system and quieter nuclear-missile submarines”*.

I'm not quite sure what this is supposed to prove. China is already a nuclear-armed state, and what Russia seems to be doing here is mainly helping China improve its existing nuclear capabilities. Such technological advances could certainly affect the nuclear balance. But why would that necessarily make them something particularly evil?

The article then describes Russia and China as *“enabling North Korea’s drive to become a fully fledged nuclear power”*.

Well, I'm not entirely sure what Russia has been doing recently. But as far as China is concerned, I'd really like to know what *The Economist* means by "helping."

As I remember it, China was not particularly supportive of North Korea's nuclear weapons development in the past, and the issue even caused diplomatic friction between the two countries. For example, after North Korea conducted its sixth nuclear test in 2017, China voted in favor of UN Security Council Resolution 2375, which imposed additional sanctions on North Korea.

Of course, that doesn't prove China maintains exactly the same position today. My guess is that China may now be more tolerant of North Korea's possession of nuclear weapons, or at least less openly opposed to it than before. But **tolerating or tacitly accepting something is not quite the same as actively helping it develop.** If *The Economist* wants to make that claim, I think it would be better to provide some concrete evidence explaining what exactly this "help" consists of.

And now we come to my least favorite part: historical analogies.

<figure>
  <img src="/individual-blog/images/evil-04-historical-analogy.png" alt="Figure 4: The Economist comparing the four-country relationship to pre-war Axis powers and mafia bosses" />
  <figcaption>Figure 4. The Economist's historical analogy to the Axis powers before World War II.</figcaption>
</figure>

The article suggests that a more appropriate historical analogy for cooperation among China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea might be the Axis powers before the outbreak of World War II. In other words, the comparison seems to imply that cooperation among these countries today resembles the formation of the German-Italian-Japanese Axis, potentially threatening the international order and even bringing to mind the possibility of another world war.

I really don't see what makes this analogy so "appropriate." If coordinating foreign policy, intelligence, and military affairs is enough to justify comparing a group of countries to the Axis powers before World War II, then I find that standard rather strange. After all, don't NATO countries and the Five Eyes intelligence alliance engage in similar forms of coordination? Yet somehow they are the righteous Allies, while we are the evil Axis. I'm having a hard time following the logic here.

The misuse of historical analogies is an interesting subject in its own right, though. Perhaps I'll write something about it another time.

Finally, there's one more argument (Figure 5): these countries are considered an axis of evil because they share a common ambition to challenge American global leadership, disrupt the US-led international order, and benefit from doing so.

Well, I suppose this argument makes some sense, provided we assume that the US-led international order is perfectly just and beyond reproach, and that challenging it is therefore evil.

I don't particularly want to get into questions of international order and justice here. But I do find it interesting to imagine what would happen if we reversed this logic and used it to explain why the United States and other Western countries are righteous.

For example, we could say that the United States and its Western partners have formed a **"League of Justice"** because they share a common ambition: to preserve American global leadership and the existing international order, while consolidating American influence in their respective regions!

Doesn't that sound so much more righteous?
