I was reading *The Economist* when I came across an article titled [*A new axis of malice threatens the global order*](https://www.economist.com/interactive/international/2026/11/19/a-new-axis-of-malice-threatens-the-global-order). Judging by the title, the article seemed to suggest that China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea were forming some kind of inherently evil and unjust partnership, one that ought to be condemned and crushed by all the righteous people of the world.

To be fair, the title did make me a little uncomfortable. After all, as a Chinese person, seeing my country labeled a member of an "axis of evil" is bound to feel somewhat offensive. It almost suggests that I, too, have somehow become part of this "evil." But I don't consider myself, or my country, evil.

Apart from feeling uncomfortable, though, I was also puzzled by something else: what exactly is "new" about this axis? As far as I remember, Western media and scholars have been grouping China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea together under the label "axis of evil" for quite some time. The term has been around for at least two or three years. For example, back in October 2023, US Senator Mitch McConnell publicly referred to these four countries as an "axis of evil" ([CBS interview transcript](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/mitch-mcconnell-senate-minority-leader-face-the-nation-transcript-10-22-2023/)). I find it hard to believe that the authors at *The Economist* were unaware of this.

Anyway, I wanted to take a closer look at exactly why *The Economist* considers these countries, including China, to be "evil."

<figure>
  <img src="/individual-blog/images/evil-02-cooperation.png" alt="Figure 2: The Economist on the four countries' military and economic co-operation" />
  <figcaption>Figure 2. The Economist on military, technological, and economic cooperation among the four countries.</figcaption>
</figure>

Well... judging by this passage (Figure 2), *The Economist* seems to suggest that one important reason this axis is evil is that these countries have actually dared to come together, share military technology and expertise, deepen their economic and industrial cooperation, and thereby shift the military balance in Europe, the Middle East, and Asia.

I'm not entirely convinced by this argument. For example, I imagine China and other countries would be more than happy if the United States were willing to share its advanced military technology and expertise with them, such as advanced fighter jets, long-range missiles, and military chips. After all, these technologies are pretty cool, and they would certainly help strengthen a country's defense and military capabilities. The problem, of course, is that the United States is clearly not very interested in sharing these things with China. In fact, it has also told other countries not to do so.

The economic side seems rather similar. Remember how the Trump administration handled tariffs on China in 2025? I wouldn't exactly call that an encouraging signal for greater economic cooperation.

So, from a certain perspective, I suspect that one reason China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea have been strengthening their military, technological, and economic cooperation is precisely that Western countries are not particularly interested in cooperating with them. Of course, this may be only one of several reasons. But if you refuse to cooperate with these countries while also portraying their cooperation with one another as somehow evil, the message seems to be: You're not allowed to cooperate with us, and you'd better not cooperate among yourselves either. Just fend for yourselves, or better yet, cease to exist.

That's why I find this argument so incredibly twisted.

As for shifting the balance of power... hmm... I suspect part of the argument concerns the regional balance between Israel and Iran. I don't really think Russia and China have done anything particularly evil in this regard. Still, I imagine the argument might be that they have supplied Iran with military equipment, components, or technical assistance, and I'm not denying that possibility.

But here's the thing. First, is Israel really some poor, helpless little country receiving no assistance from Western powers? I hardly think so. The United States has long provided Israel with substantial military assistance, including advanced weapons and cooperation on missile defense.

I suppose someone might respond by saying that Israel represents the forces of good, while Iran represents the forces of evil. Therefore, helping the good is good, and helping the evil is evil. Well, if that's the argument, I guess there's not much left for me to say.

Second, before the issue of Iran, there was also the issue of Iraq. Back then, the region arguably had something closer to a balance of power. That changed after Iraq was "liberated" in 2003, when Saddam Hussein's regime was overthrown. A regional power that had previously served as a counterweight to Iran was severely weakened, allowing Iran to expand its regional influence. This is practically a consensus in discussions of Middle Eastern geopolitics. If altering the regional balance of power is itself something worthy of condemnation, I'm not sure why the United States' actions back then seem to fall outside the same standard of judgment.

Anyway, let's see what other arguments the article has to offer.

Another argument is that this axis is changing the global nuclear balance (Figure 3).

<figure>
  <img src="/individual-blog/images/evil-03-nuclear.png" alt="Figure 3: The Economist on the global nuclear balance" />
  <figcaption>Figure 3. The Economist on the global nuclear balance.</figcaption>
</figure>

This part also seems rather questionable. The article says the Kremlin is helping China build "a missile-warning system and quieter nuclear-missile submarines", citing Western officials.

What I don't quite understand is why this means the global nuclear balance is now under threat from some kind of evil force. China is already a nuclear-armed state, and what Russia appears to be doing here is mainly helping China improve its existing nuclear technology. These technological advances could certainly affect the nuclear balance, but at least from this passage, I don't see why China's efforts to improve these capabilities should be considered particularly evil.

The article then describes Russia and China as "enabling North Korea's drive to become a fully fledged nuclear power."

Well, I'm not entirely sure what Russia has been doing recently, but I certainly don't understand why China is being described as helping North Korea become a nuclear power. As far as I remember, China has never officially expressed clear support for North Korea's development of nuclear weapons. Historically, China has not been particularly supportive of North Korea's nuclear program either, and the issue has even caused diplomatic tensions between the two countries. For example, after North Korea conducted its sixth nuclear test in 2017, China voted in favor of UN Security Council Resolution 2375, which imposed additional sanctions on North Korea.

I suppose China may have become more tolerant of North Korea's nuclear weapons development, or at least less openly opposed to it than before? I'm not entirely sure, and I doubt many readers could figure it out from that single sentence in the article. But even so, tolerating or tacitly accepting something is not quite the same as "helping develop" it. If *The Economist* wants to make that claim, I think the best approach would be to provide some concrete evidence so that I can understand what exactly this "help" is supposed to mean.

And now we come to the part I dislike the most: the historical analogy.

<figure>
  <img src="/individual-blog/images/evil-04-historical-analogy.png" alt="Figure 4: The Economist comparing the four-country relationship to pre-war Axis powers and mafia bosses" />
  <figcaption>Figure 4. The Economist's historical analogy to the Axis powers before World War II.</figcaption>
</figure>

The article suggests that a more appropriate historical comparison for the cooperation among China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea might be the Axis powers in the years leading up to the Second World War. In other words, the analogy seems to imply that the cooperation among these countries today bears some resemblance to the formation of the German-Italian-Japanese Axis, and could have similarly destructive consequences for the world order, perhaps even contributing to the outbreak of another world war.

I really don't understand what makes this analogy so "appropriate." I simply don't see anything inherently wrong with countries coordinating their foreign policies, intelligence activities, and military affairs. If several countries have common security interests, such coordination seems perfectly understandable. Don't NATO countries and the Five Eyes intelligence alliance do similar things? Yet somehow they are the righteous Allies, while we become the evil Axis powers. I really don't understand the logic here.

At least to me, this doesn't seem like a particularly good historical analogy. The misuse of historical analogies is certainly a topic worth discussing, though, and perhaps I'll write something about it another time.

Finally, there is one more argument (Figure 5): these countries are considered an evil axis because they share a common desire to challenge American global leadership, disrupt the US-led international order, and benefit from doing so.

Well, I suppose this argument makes some sense, provided that the US-led order is entirely just and absolutely flawless, and that challenging it is therefore an act of evil.

I don't particularly want to get into the question of international order and justice here. But I do find it interesting to imagine what would happen if we reversed this logic completely and used it to explain why the United States and other Western countries are righteous.

For example, we could say that the United States and other Western countries have come together to form a "Justice League" because they share a common desire: to preserve American global leadership and the existing international order, while strengthening American influence in the regions of its Western partners!

Just going by how it sounds, does that really seem so much more righteous?
