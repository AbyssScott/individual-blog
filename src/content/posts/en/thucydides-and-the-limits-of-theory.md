I really like Fisher's starting point: some of our interpretations of Thucydides' political thought may be mistaken. We sometimes attribute the views expressed by political figures in *The History of the Peloponnesian War* directly to Thucydides himself, and even use these views to determine his place in the history of political thought.

Fisher points out something interesting. There are two distinct layers of text in *The History of the Peloponnesian War*: **Thucydides' own narrative and analysis**, and **the speeches of political figures** that he records and reconstructs. Thucydides uses the term ***physis*** (nature) only five times in his own narrative, and only two of these instances refer to universal human nature. Yet strongly deterministic accounts of **human nature** appear frequently in the speeches of his political figures.

So why should we assume that what these political figures say represents the theories that Thucydides himself believed?

Well, I hadn't realized this before. Apparently, I've been unfair to Thucydides all along. My apologies, Mr. Thucydides! In my defense, I've only read a few pages of *The History of the Peloponnesian War*. It's a very long book!

## On historical analogies

Another thing I find interesting is how later thinkers have used Thucydides to explain modern politics. For quite some time, ideas drawn from his work have served as intellectual foundations for various theories in politics and international relations, some of which take a rather tragic and fatalistic view of human nature and political conflict. One prominent example is the so-called **“Thucydides's Trap.”** I imagine that anyone who has studied international relations (IR) at a university in China is familiar with this concept, as well as scholars of great-power competition such as Graham Allison, John Mearsheimer, and Robert Gilpin.

This brings me back to something I've discussed before: [**historical analogies**](/individual-blog/en/tags/Historical%20Analogy/). We often see politicians use historical analogies to explain contemporary political developments and even to predict the future. But I think many of these analogies are better understood as **heuristics** that simplify our thinking than as rigorous comparisons in the sense of academic research.

**Does the fact that two historical events share certain similarities mean that they will have similar outcomes?**

From the perspective of modern causal inference, there are several problems here.

First, how do we know that the characteristics these cases share actually caused the historical outcome? Second, how were the historical cases selected in the first place? If we focus only on cases of great-power competition that ended in war while ignoring those that did not, our understanding of the risk of war may be biased. Finally, even if we can identify a causal relationship, why should we expect it to hold across different historical and political contexts?

Here's an example I often bring up in conversations with my classmates: I suspect that Athens and Sparta (or Athens and Melos, for that matter) didn't have professional diplomatic bureaucracies in the modern sense, or diplomatic hotlines that they could use to call each other and cool things down when tensions were running high. There is no reason to assume in advance that these institutional differences make no difference to how conflicts unfold.

I have nothing against historical analogies. They can give us some interesting research hypotheses, but coming up with a hypothesis and testing it are two different things.

## On the limits of theory

Toward the end of the article, Fisher discusses another point that I particularly like: **we may mistake patterns of behavior that emerge under specific circumstances for universal features of political life.** He even extends this discussion to the Prisoner's Dilemma in game theory.

The problem isn't that we're using a simplified model. After all, models are always simplifications. The problem is whether we remember the assumptions behind the model and the circumstances under which those assumptions are reasonable.

**Abstraction and extrapolation** are, of course, necessary parts of theorizing. But if people keep expanding the explanatory scope of a theory without paying attention to the conditions under which it holds, and are unwilling to take counterexamples seriously, then what they have, it seems to me, is something closer to a **fervent belief**.

And once it becomes a matter of faith, it's pretty hard to persuade anyone otherwise. I suspect this might also help explain why people in both China and the United States, in political circles and public discussions alike, remain so fascinated by debates over the Thucydides's Trap.
